# Hook Rules Guide

## Purpose
This document defines practical hook rules for short-form ecommerce video.

A hook is the opening moment that determines whether a viewer keeps scrolling or gives the content a chance.
In short-form commerce, the hook is usually the most fragile part of the video because the viewer has not yet committed any attention.

This guide explains:
- what a good hook must accomplish
- the main types of hooks used in ecommerce video
- how to choose the right hook style
- what makes hooks fail
- how to improve hook performance in script, storyboard, and prompt workflows

---

## 1. Core Role of the Hook

The hook has one main job:

**Earn enough attention for the viewer to stay long enough to receive the selling message.**

In practice, a strong hook often needs to do at least one of these very quickly:
- signal relevance
- show a problem
- show a result
- create contrast
- trigger curiosity
- create tension
- identify the target viewer
- interrupt visual sameness in the feed

A hook does **not** need to explain the whole product.
It needs to earn the next few seconds.

---

## 2. The First 1–3 Seconds Rule

In short-form ecommerce content, the first 1–3 seconds are often the highest-risk zone.

This means the opening should usually avoid:
- long setup
- brand introduction first
- vague lifestyle framing
- broad generic talking
- slow camera movement with no clear point
- context that takes too long to decode

A viewer should quickly be able to feel one of the following:
- “This is about a problem I have.”
- “I want that result.”
- “This might be useful to me.”
- “Wait, what is happening here?”
- “That looks better than what I’m doing now.”

If the viewer feels none of these early, retention risk increases.

---

## 3. What a Good Hook Must Do

A commercially strong hook usually performs well across four dimensions:

### 3.1 Clarity
The viewer can understand what the opening is about quickly.

Questions:
- Is the first message easy to decode?
- Is the product context too hidden?
- Does the opening require too much explanation?

### 3.2 Relevance
The viewer feels the content may matter to them.

Questions:
- Is there a pain point, desire, audience callout, or use case?
- Is the opening generic or personally meaningful?

### 3.3 Tension / Curiosity / Contrast
The opening creates enough friction to keep watching.

Questions:
- Is there an unresolved question?
- Is there a noticeable before/after expectation?
- Is something surprising, inconvenient, or unusually useful?

### 3.4 Visual Support
The hook can work in a scroll-based, mobile, often-muted environment.

Questions:
- Would the opening still make sense with no sound?
- Is the visual doing enough work?
- Is text support needed?

---

## 4. Hook Evaluation Formula

A useful working formula is:

**Strong Hook = Fast Clarity + Audience Relevance + Tension/Contrast/Curiosity + Easy Visual Readability**

If two or more of these are missing, the hook is likely weak.

Example:
- Clear but not relevant → informative but skippable
- Relevant but visually flat → understood but not stopping enough
- Surprising but unclear → attention without usable meaning
- Visually strong but message-empty → watched briefly but low persuasion value

---

## 5. Main Hook Types

### 5.1 Pain-First Hook
Start with a problem, frustration, inconvenience, or unwanted condition.

Examples:
- “If your makeup has been looking dry lately…”
- “I was so tired of doing this the hard way.”
- “My skin kept looking dull no matter what I used.”

Best for:
- problem-solution products
- categories with familiar frustration
- strong audience relevance

Strength:
Fast relevance

Risk:
May feel generic if the pain point is too common or weakly phrased

---

### 5.2 Result-First Hook
Open with the result before the explanation.

Examples:
- “This is what my skin looked like right after using it.”
- “I did not expect this to make my counter look this clean.”
- “This is the easiest way I’ve found to get that glow.”

Best for:
- visually demonstrable products
- transformation products
- beauty, cleaning, organization, convenience categories

Strength:
Immediate payoff

Risk:
If the result is subtle, the hook may not feel strong enough

---

### 5.3 Audience Callout Hook
Call out a specific audience or identity group.

Examples:
- “If you have dry skin, listen to this.”
- “For anyone who’s always rushing in the morning…”
- “If you wear makeup but hate heavy skincare…”

Best for:
- niche targeting
- clear audience segmentation
- products with a specific use context

Strength:
Strong relevance filtering

Risk:
Too narrow if the audience is broader than the callout

---

### 5.4 Mistake / Correction Hook
Open by challenging a habit or exposing a mistake.

Examples:
- “You’re probably using this wrong.”
- “I stopped doing this and my skin looked way better.”
- “If you’re still doing it this way, this is easier.”

Best for:
- educational hooks
- comparison structures
- products that replace older methods

Strength:
Creates tension and authority quickly

Risk:
Can sound preachy or generic if overused

---

### 5.5 Comparison Hook
Create immediate contrast between old and new, bad and better, before and after.

Examples:
- “This is what I used to use, and this is why I switched.”
- “One side with it, one side without it.”
- “This took me two minutes before. Now it takes ten seconds.”

Best for:
- replacement products
- utility products
- performance or efficiency categories

Strength:
Fast proof structure

Risk:
Needs clear visual or logical contrast to work well

---

### 5.6 Curiosity Hook
Create a reason to stay by opening an unresolved loop.

Examples:
- “I didn’t expect this to work as well as it did.”
- “I only tried this because I was desperate.”
- “This looked so unnecessary until I actually used it.”

Best for:
- creator-led UGC
- testimonial content
- ad resistance reduction

Strength:
Soft, human, and often native-feeling

Risk:
Too vague if the payoff is delayed or weak

---

### 5.7 Reaction Hook
Lead with facial expression, emotion, or immediate creator response.

Examples:
- surprise at result
- frustration before product use
- immediate positive reaction during demo

Best for:
- UGC content
- products with sensory payoff
- creator-native formats

Strength:
Human and fast

Risk:
Weak if not supported by clear context quickly after

---

### 5.8 Visual Pattern Interrupt Hook
Use an opening image that looks different enough to interrupt the feed.

Examples:
- unexpected close-up
- awkward or messy problem state
- unusual use context
- sharp before/after contrast
- visually satisfying motion

Best for:
- crowded categories
- visually strong products
- heavily mobile-scrolled environments

Strength:
Can stop the thumb before words land

Risk:
If the visual is confusing, viewers may leave quickly anyway

---

## 6. How to Choose the Right Hook Type

Choose the hook type based on:
- product category
- audience sophistication
- proof strength
- visual demo potential
- main friction point
- platform context

### Choose pain-first when:
- the problem is common and emotionally relevant
- the product is a clear solution
- audience recognition is the fastest route to relevance

### Choose result-first when:
- the product has a visible payoff
- the result is easy to show quickly
- you want stronger scroll-stop through payoff

### Choose comparison when:
- the product clearly replaces an older method
- the difference is easy to show
- the category benefits from practical efficiency contrast

### Choose curiosity or testimonial when:
- the user needs more trust
- the product category is ad-saturated
- creator tone matters more than aggressive directness

### Choose audience-callout when:
- the target is identifiable and specific
- not every viewer should be addressed
- sharper relevance matters more than broad reach

---

## 7. Hook-to-Product Fit Rules

Not every hook fits every product.

### Strong fit examples
- visible result product → result-first
- pain-heavy utility product → pain-first
- replacement product → comparison
- skepticism-heavy beauty product → testimonial or result-first
- routine product → audience + use-case callout

### Weak fit examples
- subtle product with no visible payoff → exaggerated result-first may fail
- broad-use product with narrow audience callout → may lose volume
- low-awareness product with abstract curiosity hook → may confuse
- explanation-heavy product with pure visual hook and no context → may leak retention

Always match the hook to what the product can honestly support.

---

## 8. Hook Visual Rules

A hook is not only a line.
It is a visual event.

The opening should consider:
- what is in frame
- how quickly the viewer understands the frame
- whether the product or problem is visible
- whether the image is generic or scroll-stopping
- whether overlay text is needed
- whether sound is required for clarity

### Good hook visuals often:
- show the problem
- show the result
- show the product in context
- show a face reacting
- use a close crop for clarity
- highlight contrast
- reduce background clutter

### Weak hook visuals often:
- show nothing specific
- delay the product and the problem
- rely on talking without visual support
- begin too wide or too polished
- blend into generic creator content with no commercial cue

---

## 9. Mute Viewing Rule

Many viewers encounter short-form content without sound.

This means the hook should ideally still communicate enough through:
- facial reaction
- product or problem visibility
- on-screen text
- contrast
- action

If the opening only works with audio, its retention potential may fall.

A useful question:
**If the sound were removed, would a viewer still understand why they should keep watching?**

If not, strengthen:
- visual framing
- text overlay
- product/problem visibility
- contrast in the first shot

---

## 10. Hook Copy Rules

### Good hook copy tends to be:
- short
- specific
- speakable
- natural
- problem-aware or result-aware
- easy to pair with visuals

### Weak hook copy tends to be:
- broad
- brand-like
- overexplained
- generic
- too polished
- empty of viewer relevance

### Avoid openings like:
- “Hi guys, today I want to share…”
- “Let me introduce this amazing product…”
- “I recently discovered…”
- “This brand sent me…”

These usually waste the most valuable seconds unless the format intentionally depends on creator intimacy and the creator already has strong audience trust.

---

## 11. Hook Failure Modes

### 11.1 Too Generic
The line could apply to almost any product.

Example:
- “You need to try this.”
- “This is amazing.”
- “I’ve been loving this lately.”

Problem:
No relevance, no curiosity, no reason to stop

---

### 11.2 Too Slow
The hook takes too long to get to the point.

Problem:
The viewer leaves before relevance arrives

---

### 11.3 Too Vague
The viewer hears something interesting but cannot decode what it means.

Problem:
Attention may be captured briefly, but comprehension is weak

---

### 11.4 Too Polished
The opening feels like ad copy instead of native content.

Problem:
Trust drops, especially in UGC environments

---

### 11.5 Too Aggressive for the Proof Level
The hook overpromises more than the video can show.

Problem:
The video creates skepticism before trust is built

---

### 11.6 Visually Flat
The line may be decent, but the frame gives no added reason to stay.

Problem:
The video blends into the feed

---

## 12. Hook Improvement Tactics

### Improve clarity by:
- naming the problem faster
- reducing extra words
- making the first sentence more concrete

### Improve relevance by:
- calling out a specific pain
- naming a familiar use case
- speaking to a known audience segment

### Improve curiosity by:
- implying a payoff
- setting up a contrast
- using a mild reversal or surprise

### Improve visual strength by:
- tightening the crop
- showing the problem or result faster
- adding a short overlay
- reducing background distraction

### Improve trust by:
- using creator-native wording
- sounding less polished
- avoiding overstatement
- pairing claims with immediate proof

---

## 13. Hook Rules by Workflow Stage

### In Script Planning
The hook should:
- define the first spoken line
- align with the main promise
- be natural to say out loud
- imply what will be shown next

### In Storyboard Planning
The hook should:
- define the first shot
- identify the first visual focus
- establish whether text is needed
- support mute viewing

### In Prompt Conversion
The hook should:
- become a visually interpretable opening event
- be expressed through observable action, framing, or contrast
- not rely on abstract ad language alone

### In Content Analysis
The hook should be judged on:
- clarity
- relevance
- tension or curiosity
- visual readability
- product/problem timing

---

## 14. Practical Hook Testing Advice

When testing hooks, vary one meaningful dimension at a time where possible.

Useful test differences:
- pain-first vs result-first
- direct callout vs curiosity
- creator reaction vs product-first
- comparison vs testimonial
- text-supported vs purely visual

Watch for:
- scroll-stop performance
- early retention
- product understanding
- downstream proof support
- whether the hook attracts the right audience, not just any audience

A high-attention hook that brings in the wrong viewer can still underperform commercially.

---

## 15. Final Hook Checklist

Before approving a hook, ask:

- Is it understandable quickly?
- Does it feel relevant to the right viewer?
- Does it create enough reason to keep watching?
- Can it be supported visually?
- Does it still work on mute?
- Does it match what the product can actually prove?
- Does it feel platform-native?
- Does it avoid wasting the first few seconds?

If the answer to several of these is no, the hook likely needs revision.

---

## Final Principle
A hook is not just an opening line.

It is the first commercial promise the video makes to the viewer:
**“Stay here for one more second, because this may matter to you.”**

If the hook cannot make that promise clearly and quickly, the rest of the video may never get a chance to work.
